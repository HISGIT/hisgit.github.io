    -- Free Time, Can You Use It Well? --

 I don’t know exactly when it started, but I really have been writing in my diary every day. There were a few days when I was simply too exhausted to write anything, but I do not think that is a bad thing.
Everyone probably has days like that.

Anyway, I wanted to look back at what I had written over the past three months, polish it a little, and put my thoughts together.

Our brains probably love watching short videos. It's effortless and comfortable. Whether we like it or not, the more we watch them, the more we want to continue watching. The recommendation algorithm slowly guides us into becoming attached to them.

Even so, there are actually many things far more enjoyable than short videos. I just could not escape from that state at the time. In other words, I got trapped by the algorithm. As someone who has spent over five hours endlessly watching short videos many times, I feel that the point where you lose the ability to stop by yourself comes around the third to fifth video. Some people may still be able to escape after the tenth one, but that depends on their fitness. Nobody is in perfect state all the time, so I think most people start losing control around ten videos. Around the fifth to tenth video, you are already entering a dangerous state. Even small sounds or movements can easily pull your attention back in. But once you pass the tenth video, you may continue watching for three to five hours without stopping. Reaching the tenth video almost means “three more hours.”

Unless there is something absolutely have to do, or some emergency interrupts you, I can't stop. eyes hurt, throat becomes dry, back and shoulders fell stiff, yet still continue. I'm running on empty. While each short video is playing, a thought rises somewhere deep inside me: “This is boring. I should stop watching now.” But my hand keeps moving as if saying, “It's fine,” and continues scrolling. At that point, it no longer feels like my own hand. It moves on its own.

Several hours later, with aching eyes, a dry throat, and stiff shoulders and back, I finally manage to escape. The first thing I tell myself is, “Oh well. I lost again this time.” Then I record the date and how many hours I was consumed by short videos. My emotions and circumstances are usually almost the same, so I do not think I need to record every detail each time. Personally, I do not keep track of the number of times I win. Of course, memory of how someone overcame the urge, what thoughts helped me push through, and what inner voice guided me are probably the things shoud be shared. But most victories happen in early stage, around the first to third video. That probably happens about three times a day. So I do not think it matters much if I know the number of those easy wins. What matters is understanding why I fell into it.

When you are free, the first question becomes how you should spend your time. For people who live alone, this probably feels like the same issue as spending too much time on short videos. Before the next video, try asking yourself, “Is this video really worth watching?” If you can keep that sense of doubt and awareness, it will probably help with other problems too. If you repeat reminding yourself over and over again, one day you may naturally be tired of short videos. But honestly, it is better to change the way spending free time altogether. I would be happy if these words could help someone while they are trying to overcome this problem.

To melt away time and day, I tried studying a little bit difficult mathematics. Two hours disappeared in an instant. Time goes so fast that I barely even noticed it. But with unfamiliar mathematics, almost 90% percent of the time is spent on understanding the propositions, theorems, proofs, and conclusions. Only a small amount of time remains for practice problems, so it's not very satisfying. I couldn't enjoy it anymore, no matter how much time I put into it,so I eventually stopped.

I also tried a method where I made decisions by creating choices for myself. Instead of writing them on paper, I simply used bullet points in a reminder app. I expected writing things down on paper to feel more calming, but in the end, when it comes to convenience, smartphone is the better one.

During the first week, I tried using this method in every situation where it might be helped. Surprisingly, I gradually stopped needing to write down options at all. Just having the options laid out in front of me and recognizing that I was hesitating was enough to help me make a decision, even without using paper. Still, the phrase “situations where it seems useful” is itself vague. I even started hesitating about what kind of situations should require making choices in the first place. I adopted this method so I would stop hesitating, but instead I began hesitating about when I should use the method itself. So I stopped worrying too much about timing. The choices I once struggled with gradually disappeared, and eventually I could make decisions almost automatically, like a routine. Repetition slowly removed the hesitation. And so, problem solved... I guess.

For people who struggle with making decisions, writing down options as lists and choosing from what is directly in front of them is a very good method. But for people who tend to procrastinate, they may postpone the timing of making the decision itself, or use the fact that they already “decided” as another excuse to delay things. Because of that, this method does not work especially well for them. I really wanted to make better use of it, but before even two weeks had passed, I had already stopped using it.

Anyway, I think I'll wrap things up here.

Thank you very much for reading all the way to the end.
